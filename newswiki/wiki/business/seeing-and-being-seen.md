# [Seeing and Being Seen](https://www.sambish.com/essays/seeing/)

## Core View
- Attention is a fundamental human need, and the internet has made it easier than ever to acquire, but excessive acquisition can lead to psychological and social harm.
- The author reflects on personal experiences with attention-seeking behaviors, from childhood theatre to adult self-consciousness, suggesting a deep-seated human desire for recognition.
- [[hubs/attention-economy|Attention Economy]] is the driving force behind social media platforms, where 99.9% of content is uncompensated and generated by users seeking validation through likes, comments, and shares.
- The article identifies a 'vitamin A' analogy for attention—essential in small doses but potentially harmful in excess, mirroring the risks of overexposure to online scrutiny and mob mentality.
- The internet functions as a collective organism where mob behavior, such as rage-based content and viral outrage, can trigger mass psychological distress and disorientation in individuals.

## Beyond the Profit Motive
- Social platforms operate as conduits for user-generated content, with most content produced without compensation, driven by the need for visibility and validation.
- The author critiques the inefficiency of current systems, where engagement is rewarded through algorithms that prioritize outrage and division over meaningful discourse.
- The concept of 'engagement bait'—content designed to provoke anger and division—is highlighted as a key mechanism for virality, often at the expense of constructive dialogue.
- [[hubs/algorithmic-bias|Algorithmic Bias]] and platform design incentivize extreme content, leading to a feedback loop where outrage drives more outrage, reducing the quality of public discourse.

## Vitamin A(ttention) and Risk
- The article warns that excessive attention can permanently alter an individual's identity, akin to hypervitaminosis, and can lead to psychological trauma, especially in victims of online mobbing.
- Death threats, though often anonymous and unverified, are presented as a symptom of extreme online hostility, with the author noting that such threats are frequently exaggerated or misreported.
- The author identifies a rational game theory behind anonymous threats: zero cost to the threatener and a psychological benefit in shifting blame to a perceived 'mob' narrative.
- Victims of online harassment often lack evidence (e.g., screenshots), suggesting a deliberate effort to avoid accountability and amplify the perception of threat.

## First Taste is Free
- The author advocates for writing in obscurity, arguing that the absence of an audience prevents the need to cater to popularity and allows for more authentic, unfiltered expression.
- The experience of receiving a positive review from a stranger (e.g., a post reviewing the author's essay) triggers a desire to engage with public platforms like Reddit, Hacker News, or Substack, despite prior resistance.
- The author expresses concern about the 'survivorship bias' in online discourse—only those who succeed in gaining visibility are seen, while the majority of content is ignored or dismissed.
- The author critiques the current culture of 'clickbait' and 'ragebait' as self-defeating, arguing that such content fails to produce meaningful outcomes and only serves to deepen societal polarization.

## Key Takeaways
- Attention is a powerful, often unregulated force in modern digital economies, with significant implications for mental health and platform design.
- The majority of online content is produced without compensation, driven by the need for validation and visibility.
- Mob mentality and algorithmic amplification of outrage create a toxic feedback loop that undermines quality of public discourse.
- The risk of excessive attention—both personal and societal—is substantial, with potential for long-term psychological harm and identity erosion.
- A culture of obscurity and deliberate non-participation may preserve intellectual integrity and reduce the pressure to conform to algorithmic expectations.
